Nuances

Breaking and going back on (a pledge)

نَقَضَ · نَكَثَ

What they share

Both words mean failing to keep an obligation one has taken on, and both are borrowed from handiwork. They differ in the image: undoing what was tied or built firm, or unravelling what was spun and woven.

How to tell them apart

Breaking, undoing what was tied firm (a rope, a knot, a building) — the opposite of ‘making fast’; in the Quran a covenant, a pact, oaths, and yarn → نَقَضَ; going back on, unravelling what was woven — close to the first; in the Quran oaths, a pledge of allegiance, and promises made in distress → نَكَثَ.

نَقَضَ to break, to undo

6× in the Quran · Verb page →

According to ar-Rāġib, naqḍ is the undoing of what was bound — a rope, a knot, a building — the opposite of making fast (ibrām); from there ‘breaking a covenant’ is borrowed. The Quran itself shows the image: the woman who undoes her yarn after spinning it strong (16:92).

نَكَثَ to go back on (an oath, a pledge)

6× in the Quran · Verb page →

According to ar-Rāġib, nakṯ is the unravelling of woven cloth and yarn, close to naqḍ, and borrowed for breaking a covenant. In the Quran it is used of oaths, of a pledge of allegiance, and of a word given in distress: Pharaoh's people promised to believe as soon as the punishment was lifted, and then went back on it.
