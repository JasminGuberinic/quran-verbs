Nuances

Chiefs, masters and great men

مَلَأ · سَيِّد · كُبَرَاء

What they share

All three words speak of people who stand above others in their community. They differ in what sets them apart: a body of notables who decide together, one who manages and looks after others, or those who are great in standing and power.

How to tell them apart

A body of notables, chiefs who take counsel together — mostly a people's chiefs opposing their messenger → مَلَأ; master, lord — one who manages; also a man of virtue (John) and a husband → سَيِّد; great men — only 33:67, beside sādatanā → كُبَرَاء.

مَلَأ chiefs, council of notables

30× in the Quran · Noun page →

According to ar-Rāġib, malaʾ is a group that gathers on one view and ‘fills the eyes’ with its presence and the souls with awe. In the Quran it is mostly a people's chiefs who oppose their messenger, but also a neutral council (the Queen of Sheba, 27:29) and even the ‘Highest Chiefs’ (37:8), whom the mufassirun explain as the angels.

سَيِّد master, chief

3× in the Quran · Noun page →

According to ar-Rāġib, the sayyid is one in charge of a large body of people; one says ‘sayyid of a people’, not ‘of a garment’. Since such a man must be refined in himself, anyone virtuous is also called sayyid — so John is named (3:39); in 12:25 the husband is called sayyid for managing his wife.

كُبَرَاء great men, grandees

1× in the Quran

In the Quran only once, beside sādatanā: those great in standing and power. Ar-Rāġib cites this ayah when he explains that ‘fathers’ may also mean those who raised someone in knowledge. The word has no entry of its own in his book.
